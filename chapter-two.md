# Queen of None

## Chapter Two: Dying to Scream

I was always by her side, the queen's shadow, her silent chronicler. Protected within the bubble of her ferromagni, I moved unscathed through the chaos, my quill and parchment ever at the ready. It was my sacred duty to record every act of glory, every drop of blood spilled in her name, so that history would remember Valeska von Hohenzollern not as a tyrant, but as a force of nature. Elgrabor, that festering sore upon the land, was our first true test. A city? No, a sprawling town, bloated with vice, its walls crumbling under the weight of its own sins. Slavers' markets reeked of despair, brothels echoed with forced laughter, and the air was thick with the stench of unwashed flesh and rotting hope. Families were torn apart daily in the central square, children auctioned like livestock, the strong broken into beasts. Baron Vortig ruled with a fist of iron, his greed blinding him to the storm approaching.

The messenger had been dispatched, bearing the proclamation: bow or perish. Six days later, we arrived, our modest army of a thousand banners fluttering against the horizon. Vortig laughed from his balcony, a vain fool in embroidered robes, his belly straining, beard oiled. "A girl?" he amplified his voice with ferromagni. "Send her back to her dolls." He ordered the messenger's execution—impaled on an iron stake, proclamation nailed to his chest, blood pooling on the cobblestones. Pride had made him deaf to reason.

Valeska halted before the gates, her gown a banner of crimson, her circlet gleaming. Her voice echoed through the streets. "Citizens of Elgrabor! Abandon this pit of depravity. Flee now, and live. Join my banner, pledge loyalty, or meet your end."

Families huddled, some with consciences intact, streaming out with meager belongings. They pledged allegiance, joining our ranks as new subjects. Defiant ones spat curses—"We bow to no one!"—and our archers loosed arrows, shafts of iron piercing them where they stood. Submission or death.

The defenders mocked, believing our bluff. "What can a girl do?"

The battle ignited. Our ladders slammed against walls, swords clashed. But we were outnumbered, their ferromagni blades humming with unnatural strength. Arrows rained, spears thrust. Our men fell in waves, cries mingling with roars. Breaches widened, panic spread. Soldiers retreated, some fleeing back toward the hills. It was a rout, our failure mounting, ladders toppling, lines breaking.

Among our ranks stood Galcedric, a nameless knight of stature, who had witnessed the horrors of Valeska's fifteenth birthday—the iron throne forged in blood, her father's exile. He had pledged loyalty then, and now, on this field, he rallied others. "Fight on for our queen!" he bellowed, his voice cutting through the chaos. "We will be victorious!" His men, inspired, formed a wall around him, but the tide crushed them. Arrows pinned his comrades, swords slashed. Galcedric fought valiantly, his armor dented, blood streaming from a gash on his arm. As his men died around him, he faltered, despair creeping in. "Hold!" he cried, but the defenders advanced, laughter echoing.

Valeska watched from the rear, her face impassive. A few of our own, gripped by fear, tried to flee—cowardice in the face of odds. She gestured subtly, iron spikes erupting from the ground, impaling them without a word. Their screams were brief, bodies crumpling. It was a test: resolve or death. No acknowledgment, just silent judgment. Our army witnessed, their loyalty forged in terror and awe.

The strife was palpable. Captain Harlan shouted orders, only to be pierced by a spear, blood spraying. Sergeants rallied, but blades sliced armor. Young recruits screamed, limbs cleaved. Retreat turned to rout, men trampling each other, failure bitter.

Then, Valeska advanced. The air hummed, iron awakening. She strode forward, her power surging, teaching her army what true might meant. Bodies contorted—limbs twisted, spines cracked, arrows reversed to pierce archers. Heads driven into dirt, earth swallowing them. Her lust for battle ignited, a hunger from her father's fall. Each kill fueled her, the trace iron from the blood rising—not the blood itself, but the metallic essence, a blessing and consequence of her father's experiments. To onlookers, it seemed she observed the blood, but it was the iron coiling around her, strengthening her muscles, sharpening her senses.

Baron Vortig was the first. Hidden in his palace, he was dragged out by chains of iron, wreathing, clawing the dirt. "No! You witch!" he howled, pride shattered.

Men turned on each other, swords bending to slash comrades, armor fusing to crush. Brothers became foes, blood flowing. Mothers destroyed with husbands—families in doorways impaled by bending frames, nails embedding, beams collapsing. Homes became tombs, markets slaughterhouses, cages warping to impale.

Valeska reveled, her laughter manic. Each death's iron flowed to her, coiling like armor, fueling her frenzy. She moved like a predator, homes exploding, carts crushing vendors, bars freeing some to skewer others. Spears bent to skewer ranks, skulls crushed with debris. Her movements faster, brutal.

She leveled Elgrabor, a large town reduced to rubble. No citizen spared inside walls, few survivors changed—minds shattered, bodies broken.

Screams echoed 60 meters away, citizens begging, our men retreating in fear.

Valeska went back to the square, where Vortig lay broken. She wrapped him in chains of iron, his body a wreck, blind and deaf now, sealed from the world.

Her men, shaken, saw allegiance to the monster they served.

As she walked out, the appalled looks of new citizens met her—horror at the carnage. She trudged through bodies and blood, boots squelching, grim smile on her face.

Vortig's screams protested faintly as she dragged him.

She mounted her horse, soaked in blood from head to toe. Iron like sand swirled in the air, a red-tinted mist around her like an aura, eyes glowing with fire unseen.

The remnants of Elgrabor burned in the distance.

She addressed her remaining men and new followers: "My rule is the rule of iron—strength under a single banner. No nations, no separators, just one common banner of strength. You who stand are worthy; those who fell were meant to. For all children of our home, I will shape this world."

Vortig begged and prayed in shambles.

Onlookers—horrified or infatuated—offered praise, mothers and families raising hands, soldiers in fear or worship raising theirs.

I recorded it all, heart pounding, notes flying.

Elgrabor was no more, a warning in blood and iron.
