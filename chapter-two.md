# Queen of None

## Chapter Two: Dying to Scream

Elgrabor was a festering wound on the map of the world, a city that had long ago surrendered its soul to depravity. Nestled in the shadow of jagged mountains, its walls were stained with the rust of old blood, its towers adorned with cages where the damned swung like wind chimes in the perpetual wind. The air was thick with the stench of unwashed bodies and despair, the streets a labyrinth of slave markets where men, women, and children were bartered like livestock. Whips cracked like thunder through the alleys, and the cries of the tormented echoed endlessly, a symphony of suffering that never ceased. Elgrabor thrived on suffering—its barons grew fat on the backs of the enslaved, their palaces built from the bones of the broken. Citizens moved through the streets with dead eyes, numb to the horrors they inflicted and endured. Slave auctions happened daily in the central square, where families were torn apart, children sold for a pittance, and the strong reduced to beasts of burden. Brothels lined the outskirts, filled with the unwilling, their doors guarded by iron chains that clanked like chains of fate. It was a place where innocence was currency, and mercy a forgotten word. No wonder the proclamation had been sent; no wonder it had been met with scorn.

The messenger arrived on horseback, his cloak bearing the Hohenzollern crest, his face pale from the long ride through hostile lands. He dismounted in the central square, the proclamation clutched in his trembling hand. "Hear the word of Queen Valeska von Hohenzollern!" he declared, his voice rising above the murmurs of the crowd and the distant wails from the cages. "She claims dominion over all lands. Bow or perish. In five nights, she will conquer Elgrabor as proof of her might." The crowd jeered, slaves in chains rattling their irons in mockery. Baron Vortig, watching from his balcony, laughed—a deep, booming sound that echoed through the square. "A girl?" he scoffed, his voice amplified by ferromagni. "Send her back to her dolls." With a wave of his hand, he ordered the messenger seized. The man struggled as guards dragged him to an iron stake in the center. "For your insolence," Vortig proclaimed, "you die." The execution was swift and brutal: a spike driven through his heart, his body left to rot as a warning, the proclamation nailed to his chest like a mockery. Blood pooled on the cobblestones, staining the stones that had seen so much cruelty.

Baron Vortig was a vain and prideful man, his body a testament to excess—belly straining against embroidered robes, rings on every finger, beard oiled and curled. He had always resisted Graf Albrecht unless the deals bent in his favor: taxes lowered for his coffers, slaves gifted for his markets, territories ceded for his expansion. Pride blinded him; he saw Valeska as a weak link, a child to be crushed. "She underestimates me at her peril," he muttered to his advisors, dismissing the threat.

Six days passed, the air growing heavier with anticipation. Whispers of Valeska's proclamation spread, but few believed. Elgrabor's defenses were formidable—walls of stone reinforced with iron, guards armed with ferromagni-enhanced blades, archers atop towers with bows that could pierce armor. The city was a fortress of vice, its people hardened by cruelty. When the Eisenburg army appeared on the horizon—a modest force of a thousand men, banners fluttering—Vortig laughed again from his tower. "Outnumbered three to one, and led by a child? This will be a feast of blood."

Valeska rode at the head, her gown fluttering like a banner of blood, her circlet gleaming. She halted before the gates, her voice amplified by ferromagni to echo through the streets. "Citizens of Elgrabor! You have one opportunity to turn away from your crimes and abandon this city of slavers and depravity. Leave now, and live."

A hush fell over the square. Families huddled in doorways, mothers clutching children, fathers weighing their sins. Some—those with consciences still intact—gathered their meager belongings and streamed out through the gates. Valeska's army parted for them, but she addressed them directly. "You who flee: join my city, become citizens of Eisenburg. Pledge loyalty, and be spared. Refuse, and meet your end."

Most agreed, their voices trembling as they swore allegiance, joining the ranks as new subjects. But a few, defiant to the last, spat curses. "We bow to no one!" they shouted. Valeska's archers, positioned on the hills, loosed arrows at her command. Shafts of iron pierced the unloyal, their bodies crumpling where they stood. The lesson was clear: submission or death.

The remaining citizens stayed, believing her bluff. "What can a girl do?" they mocked from the walls.

The battle erupted. Valeska's army charged, ladders slamming against the walls, swords clashing with spears. But they were outnumbered, the defenders fierce and desperate. Arrows rained down, spears thrust from embrasures. Eisenburg soldiers fell in waves, their cries mingling with the defenders' roars. Valeska watched from the rear, her face impassive. "Fight," she commanded her generals. "I will join when needed."

The tide turned against them. Breaches opened in the lines, soldiers retreating under the onslaught. Vortig's forces poured out, encircling the invaders. Panic spread—Eisenburg men broke ranks, some trying to flee back toward the hills. But it was too late; Valeska's power had awakened. The air hummed, iron in the earth responding. Bodies twisted mid-charge, limbs bending at unnatural angles, spines cracking as if invisible hands wrung them like rags. Soldiers on both sides screamed, their forms contorting into ungodly shapes—heads lolling on necks bent backward, arms fused to torsos in grotesque embraces. Arrows loosed from bows froze in midair, their iron shafts refusing to fly, then reversed, piercing the archers' own throats. Heads were driven into the dirt, soil swallowing them up to the shoulders as if the earth itself rebelled.

Valeska's violence escalated with each step. She became a whirlwind of fury, her ferromagni lashing out indiscriminately. An Eisenburg soldier blocked her path, his sword raised in confusion. Iron spikes erupted from the ground, impaling him, his blood spraying as he gasped his last. Another, fleeing the chaos, was caught in a wave of twisting metal, his armor fusing to his skin in a cage of pain. Her own troops, caught in the crossfire, suffered too—those who stood in her way were crushed, their loyalty no shield against her wrath. She was beyond control, her power a living entity, hungry and merciless.

Any person in the city was at her mercy. Homes collapsed as iron beams bent inward, crushing families beneath rubble. Streets ran red, bodies piled like refuse. From over sixty meters away, the blood-curdled screams could be heard—piercing, unending wails that chilled the blood. Citizens begged, pleaded, but mostly screamed: "Mercy! Please!" Children wailed, men howled, women sobbed. Even Valeska's own men inside the city retreated from the horror, their faces pale, their swords dropping as they fled the walls, leaving the queen alone amidst the devastation.

Baron Vortig was captured alive, dragged before her in chains. As an example, Valeska rendered him blind and deaf—iron threads weaving into his eyes and ears, sealing his senses forever. "You will follow me," she said, "blind and deaf, for all your days." He was made a servant of the crown, sent to the pits—the most vile place, once her father's lab where genetic experiments twisted creatures and souls alike. Now a run-down cellar of filth, filled with vile abominations: mutated beasts with extra limbs, gibbering wretches from failed breedings, broken souls driven mad. With no defenses, blind and deaf, Vortig was tortured endlessly—clawed by creatures, tormented by the insane, his screams unheard in the darkness.

When it ended, silence fell, broken only by the drip of blood and the groans of the dying. No one survived unchanged; those few who escaped the walls—wounded stragglers—bore scars not just of flesh, but of soul. Their eyes hollow, their minds shattered by what they had witnessed. Valeska stood in the center of the ruin, her gown stained crimson, her breath steady. She had laid waste almost alone, her power a testament to dominance.

As she walked out through the shattered gates, a tidal wave of iron followed—blood and metal coalescing into a river of snakes, clinging to her shadow, slithering at her heels like loyal beasts. The world had seen her might, and trembled.

I, the scribe, recorded it all from the camp, my quill trembling. Elgrabor was no more, a warning etched in blood.
