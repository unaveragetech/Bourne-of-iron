### Bourne of Iron: Book One - Synopsis of the First Half

**Prologue: The Stormborn Cry**

The castle of Eisenburg loomed against the raging tempest, its stone towers piercing the blackened sky like iron spears. Thunder cracked overhead, echoing through the cavernous halls where ancestral tapestries fluttered in the drafts from arrow-slit windows. Candles guttered in wrought-iron sconces along the walls of the birthing chamber, casting flickering shadows that danced like restless spirits on the bloodstained linens. Rain lashed the leaded glass panes, and the wind howled through the cracks, carrying the scent of wet earth and ozone. Inside, the air was thick with the metallic tang of blood, the sharp herbal fumes of midwives' potions, and the primal cries of labor. Graf Albrecht von Eisen, lord of the domain, paced the shadowed perimeter of the room, his boots echoing on the cold flagstone floor. His face, etched with lines of worry and anticipation, reflected the storm's fury—dark eyes narrowed, jaw clenched beneath a beard streaked with premature gray. He had sired many children before, but this one... this one felt different, a burgeoning storm within the storm without.

From the maid's perspective—Elsa, a young woman with calloused hands from years of scrubbing floors and tending hearths—she knelt at the bedside, her simple woolen dress soaked with sweat and fluids. She had been summoned in haste from the kitchens, her heart pounding not just from the exertion of aiding the countess's labor, but from the raw power unfolding before her. The countess, pale and exhausted, gripped the iron bedposts with white-knuckled hands, her body wracked by contractions that seemed to shake the very foundations of the castle. Elsa wiped the brow of her lady with a damp cloth, whispering encouragements she barely believed herself. She had seen births before—messy, painful affairs—but this one carried an unnatural weight, as if the infant within thrummed with an energy that made the air hum and her skin prickle. As the countess screamed anew, Elsa reached out instinctively, her fingers brushing the silver rings on her own hands—simple heirlooms from her mother, etched with runes for protection against evil spirits. They warmed against her skin, vibrating faintly, as if alive.

Albrecht watched from afar, his noble blood demanding detachment, yet his paternal instincts roared like the thunder outside. He had married for alliance, not love, but this child—prophesied by seers as a harbinger of power—stirred something primal in him. The countess's cries grew sharper, and Elsa leaned in closer, her hands steadying the emerging form. With a final, guttural push, Valeska entered the world—a tiny, wriggling bundle of flesh and fury, her cries piercing the chamber like shards of glass. From Elsa's view, the baby's eyes opened briefly, glowing with an otherworldly light, and a wave of heat washed over her. Valeska's emotions, raw and newborn, surged outward: a spark of joy at the touch of air, mingled with the shock of cold, igniting her latent power. Elsa felt it first—a tingling in her fingertips where they touched the infant's skin, as if lightning coursed through her veins.

Albrecht saw it too, his eyes widening in awe and calculation. This was no ordinary child; her power was instant, tied inextricably to her emotions, making her a force of nature even as a babe. Unprovoked, she was calm, but provoked by the slightest discomfort, she could unleash devastation. He stepped forward, his hand outstretched not to help, but to claim this weapon of flesh and iron will. Elsa, sensing the shift, recoiled instinctively, her body tensing as fear gripped her—fear of the unknown, of the power she now cradled. In her panic, she clutched Valeska tighter to her chest, her posture arching protectively, the rings on her fingers pressing inward like claws. The runes flared, channeling the baby's emotional surge, and in that moment of tension—maid's recoil meeting father's advance—the rings exploded. Shrapnel burst outward in a hail of molten silver, propelled by Valeska's provoked fury. Shards tore through Elsa's gown, embedding in her flesh, her screams joining the storm as blood sprayed across the linens. She crumpled to the floor, her life ebbing away in crimson pools, her eyes wide with betrayal and terror. Valeska's cries intensified, her power feeding on the chaos, a whirlwind of emotion that rattled the chamber's iron fixtures and extinguished candles in puffs of smoke.

Albrecht stood frozen, unharmed by the blast, his gaze fixed on his daughter. He did not rush to Elsa's side—there was no time for mercy, no room for weakness. Instead, he saw Valeska for what she was: a weapon forged in iron and emotion, unstoppable if roused, a tool to reshape the world. The storm outside raged on, but within the castle, a new tempest had been unleashed. Elsa's death was but the first ripple of Valeska's impact, a testament to how even as an infant, her presence could shatter lives.

In the shadowed annals of this world—a twisted mirror of the one you might know, where empires rise and fall not on the steam of factories or the crackle of electricity, but on the raw, unyielding pull of iron and blood—the story begins with me. Valeska von Hohenzollern, born in the waning years of the 18th century, when the old kings still clung to their thrones and the whispers of revolution stirred like dust in forgotten chambers. This timeline diverges from yours in subtle ways: no grand Industrial Revolution here to birth machines that roar and spew smoke. Innovation lags, trapped in the forge of alchemy and the crucible of human ambition. Instead, a mutation courses through the veins of the elite, a gift—or curse—called ferromagni. It grants the ability to bend metals to one's will, to command iron as if it were an extension of flesh and fury. Most who bear it wield it modestly: a knight might sharpen his blade with a thought, a smith might hammer without tools. But I... I am the outlier, the aberration. My power is unparalleled, a storm that devours the ordinary and leaves devastation in its wake. And this is my tale, told from the depths of my bloodthirsty soul, where love is a lie and power is the only truth.

I remember my birth as if it were yesterday, though I was but a squalling infant in that cursed chamber. The air reeked of iron and blood, the torchlight flickering like dying embers on walls that seemed to pulse with life. My father, Graf Albrecht von Hohenzollern, cradled me in his arms, his face a mask of grim determination. He was no mere noble; he was a general of the Prussian armies, a man who had carved his name into history with campaigns that drenched fields in gore. But beneath the uniform and medals beat the heart of a fanatic, a genetics enthusiast obsessed with domination. He saw the world as a breeding ground, where the strong devoured the weak, and he sought to unlock the secrets of heredity to forge an unbreakable lineage. In me, he believed he had found his masterpiece—a daughter who would surpass all others in ferromagni power. Yet even then, as my cries pierced the night, I sensed his coldness, the way his eyes gleamed not with paternal warmth but with calculation. Love? In his world, it was a weakness, a chain to be broken. And so, from my first breath, he warped me, teaching me that affection was folly, that true strength lay in the lust for power alone.

The midwife approached, her hands trembling as she reached for me. "Give her to me," she whispered, her voice laced with fear. She wore rings of cold iron, symbols of her station, and as she brushed one against my cheek, the metal stirred. It twisted like a living thing, jaws snapping shut on her finger. Bone shattered with a sickening crack, and her scream was choked off as the other rings erupted into barbs, embedding themselves in her throat. Blood sprayed across the tapestries, staining the royal griffins crimson. She fell, choking on her own life, and in that moment, I felt a surge within me—a primal hunger, not for milk, but for the chaos my power unleashed. The world was cruel, I realized even then, and power was the only shield against its teeth.

Ser Dietrich, my father's loyal knight of the Magnetic Rose—a brotherhood sworn to contain such aberrations—lunged for his sword. But steel rebelled against him. His gauntlet clasped the hilt, and the blade fused to its sheath, the pommel melting into a searing pool that burned his thigh. He tore it free, shards digging into his flesh, and backed away, muttering prayers. "This is no child," he gasped. "This is a weapon." His armor softened under my gaze, as if my tiny eyes held the forge's heat. The castle itself groaned in protest, stained-glass windows shattering inward, shards swirling in eddies that pinned a servant boy through the eye. He crumpled, lifeless, and I felt no pity—only a thrill at the dominion I commanded. My father pressed me closer, his breastplate warping against his chest, metal groaning like a beast in agony. "She's only frightened," he lied, his voice unsteady. But I knew the truth: this was no mere cry. It was a storm, a herald of my unbridled might.

The chandelier plummeted, igniting flames that danced across the rushes. Dietrich fled, his armor glowing red-hot, leaving us alone. My father knelt beside a cradle whose iron frame writhed like serpents, and I grasped his signet ring. The Hohenzollern eagle melted, talons twisting into claws. He named me Valeska, after the iron-witch of legend, and in that name, I felt the weight of destiny. Below, in a sealed smithy, an anvil vibrated, as if the earth itself acknowledged my arrival.

Weeks passed, and my father secluded himself in his study, poring over fragments of the iron that had borne witness to my birth—the midwife's rings, the chandelier's remnants, his own twisted breastplate. He invited the metal's hum into his veins, watching crimson light seep into his fingers. "Carefully," he murmured to himself. "Patiently." He was no fool; he knew the Brotherhood would come, as foretold, their lead-lined chains clanking at the gates of Königsberg Castle. But he saw opportunity in me, a chance to breed a dynasty of dominators. Genetics fascinated him, the way traits passed like heirlooms. He would experiment, unlock my secrets, and use them to conquer. Yet his experiments were cold, clinical. He raised me not as a daughter, but as a subject. Affection was withheld, replaced by drills in power control. "Love is illusion," he told me as I grew, his voice devoid of warmth. "It binds the weak. Power frees the strong." I learned early that vulnerability was death, that to survive this cruel world, I must embrace the lust for dominance. His attitude warped me, twisting any nascent softness into steel. By age five, I could levitate iron filings with a glance, mold them into weapons. But he pushed me harder, his eyes gleaming with ambition. "You will be greater than I," he said, and I hungered for it, my bloodthirsty nature awakening with each triumph.

My childhood in Königsberg was a blur of isolation and intensity. The castle, perched on the banks of the Pregel River, was a fortress of stone and secrets, its towers scraping the Prussian sky. The year was 1798, and the world outside simmered with unrest—the echoes of the French Revolution rippling eastward, though here, kings still reigned absolute. Technology lagged; no railroads snaked across the land, no telegraphs hummed with messages. Instead, ferromagni users like my father commanded armies, bending cannons to their will in battles that bled the earth. I was kept hidden, a prodigy in the making. My father, the general, returned from campaigns with tales of conquest, but his true passion lay in the lab he built beneath the castle. There, amid bubbling vials and iron apparatuses, he studied genetics—crossing bloodlines, infusing ferromagni through selective breeding. "Domination is hereditary," he preached, forcing me to watch as he dissected subjects, human and otherwise, to isolate the mutation. His coldness seeped into me; when he praised my progress, it was not with pride, but with the detachment of a scientist noting a successful formula. Love? I saw it as weakness, a tool the feeble used to manipulate the mighty. In my eyes, the world was a feast, and power the knife to carve it.

By ten, I surpassed him in ferromagni strength. He could manipulate iron at a distance of meters; I could command it across rooms, twisting swords into knots or hurling nails like arrows. His experiments intensified, injecting me with elixirs meant to amplify my abilities. "You must be fifteen times my power," he calculated, his voice a chill wind. "Then you will lead the charge against our enemies." I embraced it, my bloodthirsty urges growing. Sparring with iron constructs, I reveled in the destruction, the way metal screamed under my will. Servants whispered of my savagery; one stable boy who dared mock me found his horseshoes fusing to his feet, dragging him into the mud. I laughed, feeling alive in the carnage. My father approved, his warped teachings taking root. "Mercy is for the defeated," he said, and I nodded, seeing the world through his lens—a cruel tapestry where power lust was the only virtue.

As I approached fifteen, the gap widened. My power was not just strength; it was finesse, intuition. I could sense iron's pulse, bend it invisibly, or erupt it in cataclysmic fury. My father, now in his fifties, grew envious. His campaigns had taken toll; scars crisscrossed his body, and his ferromagni waned. I overtook him by fifteen-fold, as he had predicted, but his pride curdled into fear. "You are a monster," he muttered one night, watching me reshape a cannonball into a sculpture of his likeness, twisted in agony. "I created you, but you threaten to consume me." The Brotherhood's scouts had come, as foretold, their lead-shielded wagons rumbling at the gates. They demanded I be chained, studied, perhaps executed. My father barred them, but his facade cracked. In his study, amid the hum of iron relics, he confessed his plan: to breed me with a rival house, dilute my power, or—worse—end me before I eclipsed him utterly.

The attempt came on my fifteenth birthday, a stormy eve in 1803. The castle thrummed with tension; outside, Prussian soldiers drilled under my father's command, their muskets glinting in the rain. He lured me to the smithy, the anvil still vibrating faintly from my birth. "A final test," he said, his eyes cold as the metal he wielded. He activated a trap—iron chains hidden in the walls, laced with poisons to dull ferromagni. They snaked toward me, hungry jaws snapping. But my power surged; I sensed the iron's betrayal, twisted it against him. The chains turned, wrapping around my father instead. He fought, his own abilities flaring, but I was fifteen times stronger. I could have killed him then, let the metal crush his bones, revel in his blood soaking the forge. My bloodthirsty nature roared for it—the world was cruel, and he had taught me cruelty. Yet something held me back, a flicker of the warped love he had instilled, or perhaps pity for the creator who feared his creation.

He gasped, pinned against the anvil, the chains tightening. "Valeska... you surpass me," he wheezed. "But power corrupts. I tried to save you—from yourself." I released him, the chains retracting like serpents. He slumped, defeated, his cold attitude shattered. From that day, my view of love deepened in distortion; it was not affection, but control. I exiled him to a remote estate, where he could plot no more. Alone in Königsberg, I embraced my destiny. The Brotherhood retreated, wary of my might. Whispers spread of the Iron Witch, and suitors from distant houses sought my hand—not for love, but for alliance. I spurned them, seeing only pawns in the game of power.

The world outside grew restless. Napoleon Bonaparte's shadow loomed over Europe, his armies marching with bayonets and cannons. In this timeline, ferromagni generals like my father tipped the scales; Prussia's defenses relied on magnetic fields to repel grapeshot, bending bullets mid-flight. Yet innovation stagnated— no steam engines revolutionized transport, only alchemical forges powered by ferromagni elite. I trained in secret, honing my abilities. My bloodthirsty urges drove me to hunt poachers in the forests, impaling them on their own traps. The cruel world fueled my lust; power was survival, and I craved more.

As the first half of my tale unfolds, I stand on the precipice of greater chaos. The Brotherhood plots anew, their lead chains forging in hidden monasteries. My father, broken but scheming, sends emissaries begging reconciliation. And within me, the storm builds—a power unparalleled, hungry for dominion. This is my world: a cruel arena where love is a weapon, and power the only victor. I am Valeska, and I will bend it to my will—or shatter it entirely.
