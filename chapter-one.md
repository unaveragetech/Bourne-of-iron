# Queen of None

## Chapter One: The Iron Throne

The great hall of Eisenburg Castle had never known such silence. It was a cavernous space, its vaulted ceiling lost in shadows, where the echoes of generations past whispered through the stone. Tapestries depicting Hohenzollern victories—armored knights bending cannons with outstretched hands—hung heavy on the walls, their colors muted by the dim light filtering through narrow slits. The air was thick with the scent of aged wood and cold iron, a reminder of the power that coursed through these halls. Candles flickered in wrought-iron sconces, their flames dancing like nervous courtiers. I stood in the recesses, my back against the rough-hewn wall, my quill poised over parchment though I had not yet written a word. My name was irrelevant; I was merely the observer, the one who saw everything and said nothing, unless spoken to. And today, I saw the beginning of something terrible.

Valeska von Hohenzollern sat upon the throne, a seat of blackened iron forged in the fires of her ancestors. She was fifteen, a slip of a girl with eyes that pierced like forged steel, her dark hair pinned back with a circlet of twisted thorns that seemed to writhe of their own accord. Her gown was simple, a deep crimson that clung to her slender form, chosen not for beauty but for the iron threads woven into the fabric—a subtle declaration of her power. The ferromagni ran strong in her blood, a mutation that allowed her to command metals as if they were extensions of her will. Most in the court possessed it in meager doses: a lord might sharpen a blade with a thought, a smith might mend a horseshoe without hammer. But Valeska... she was the storm incarnate, her abilities unparalleled, tied inextricably to her emotions. Unprovoked, she was calm, a still pool. Provoked, she became devastation.

The hall was filled with the elite of Eisenburg: nobles in velvet and fur, advisors with quills and ledgers, guards in armor that gleamed with magnetic wards. They had come for her first true day in court, the formal assumption of power following her father's exile. Graf Albrecht von Hohenzollern, the Prussian general who had ruled with an iron fist, was gone—banished to a remote estate after the confrontation on her fifteenth birthday. The details were whispered in the corridors: the smithy trap, the chains that turned against him, his defeat at the hands of his own creation. "He created a monster," the servants said, and I wondered if they were right. But today was not for whispers; today was for fealty.

Valeska's voice cut through the silence, steady and low. "We gather to affirm the order of Eisenburg. My father's policies have bled the land dry, his alliances sold for gold. We will rebuild on firmer ground."

She paused, her gaze sweeping the room like a blade. "Bow now, and acknowledge your queen. Those who bow freely shall be spared the force. Those who resist... will submit nonetheless."

Murmurs rippled through the crowd, a wave of unease. Baron Klaus, a stout man with a face like weathered granite and a beard flecked with gray, stepped forward. He was one of the old guard, a landowner whose estates bordered the castle grounds, his ferromagni modest but his tongue sharp. "My Lady," he said, his voice booming, "you are young. Scarcely more than a child. How can we trust the rule of one who has not seen the winters of governance?"

The tension in the hall thickened, like fog before a gale. Eyes darted to Valeska, watching for the flicker of anger. I felt it too, a hum in the air, as if the iron fixtures—the sconces, the railings—vibrated faintly in anticipation.

Valeska's gaze fixed on him, her expression unchanging. "Age is not the measure of wisdom, Baron. Experience is."

Klaus puffed out his chest, his armor creaking. "Experience? What experience have you, save the nursery and the forge? Your father ruled for decades, and now you cast him aside on a whim. The people whisper of instability. Of danger."

A low murmur agreed with him, heads nodding. Lady Helga, the visiting baroness in her silks, shifted uncomfortably. Ser Dietrich, the knight who had fled her birth, stood rigid near the throne, his hand on his sword hilt.

Valeska leaned forward slightly, her fingers tracing the armrests of the throne—iron that bent like clay under her touch, though she did not yet command it. "Whispers are the tools of the weak, Baron. Speak plainly, or hold your tongue."

Klaus's face reddened, his ferromagni flaring in his eyes—a faint glow, the sign of metal manipulation. "Plainly? You are a child playing at king! Your power is a curse, not a gift. We have heard the tales—of the maid at your birth, the servants twisted in agony. How long before you turn on us?"

The words hung in the air, sharp as daggers. The crowd held its breath. I saw the shift in Valeska's eyes, a darkening, like clouds gathering over the moors. The tension escalated slowly, deliberately, as if the hall itself breathed in anticipation.

"Baron Klaus," Valeska said, her voice thoughtful, almost curious, "why would you wish this fate upon yourself? Why provoke what you cannot withstand?"

Klaus sneered, his hubris blinding him. "I'll never bow to a Witch!" he exclaimed, his voice echoing off the walls, a declaration of defiance that sealed his doom.

The words pushed Valeska over the edge. Her calm shattered, replaced by a cold fury. The air hummed. Valeska's power surged, invisible but palpable, a wave of heat that made the iron in the hall sing. The sconces trembled, their flames leaping higher. Baron Klaus's armor, laced with iron studs, began to shift—subtly at first, a buckle loosening, a plate warping.

"You have chosen," Valeska said, her tone devoid of emotion. The escalation was inevitable. Valeska's eyes narrowed, and with a flick of her will, the baron's armor came alive. The iron plates twisted inward, coiling around his limbs like serpents, piercing his flesh but not fatally. His body convulsed, muscles spasming as the metal fused to his nerves, paralyzing him. He could not speak, his jaw locked in a silent scream, his eyes wide with terror—mentally alive, aware of every agonizing second, but unable to move or utter a sound. He slumped to the floor, a living statue, his mind trapped in a cage of iron will.

The court gasped, recoiling in horror. But Valeska was not done. Justice would be meted out to more than just the baron. Her gaze swept the room, landing on Ser Dietrich, her father's right hand, the knight who had served Albrecht loyally. "You stood by him," she said softly, "and watched as he fell. Loyalty to the old order is treason to the new."

Dietrich fell to his knees, his voice breaking. "Mercy, my Lady! I beg forgiveness! I was loyal to your father, but I see now—I submit!"

His pleas were lost on Valeska. She was beyond pity, her heart hardened by the lust for power. She exercised her will freely, without remorse. Dietrich's armor glowed, then twisted. Chains of iron extruded from the floor, wrapping around him, fusing his limbs into stillness. He fell to his knees, paralyzed like Klaus, his eyes pleading, his mind intact but his body a prison.

The hall erupted in panic, nobles backing away, some crying out. Valeska rose, her power extending like tendrils. The iron in their blood—traces in every vein—responded. Thin streams of metallic essence flowed from the wounds of the paralyzed men, snaking through the air like crimson serpents, drawn to her as if all iron recognized its true master. The blood-iron coiled around her feet, then up her gown, infusing her with a glow that made her eyes blaze. "All iron is mine to command," she whispered, and the hall's fixtures shuddered in acknowledgment.

She turned to the crowd, her voice cold. "Bow. Acknowledge your queen."

Resistance flickered—Lord Voss hesitated, Frau Greta clutched her cross—but Valeska's will forced them. Invisible hands of iron bent their knees, pressed their heads down. Those who bowed freely were spared the full force, their submission voluntary. Those who resisted were compelled, their bodies bent against their will. One by one, they bowed, compelled, their wills broken by the demonstration of power.

As she exited, the iron serpents followed, slithering at her heels, a testament to her dominion.

In the courtyard outside, Baron Klaus's son—a young heir of sixteen, brash and defiant—confronted her. "You monster!" he spat, echoing his father's doubts. "What have you done to him?"

Valeska regarded him calmly, not angry, but resolute. She must make an example of lineage, the price of defiance. "Your father's hubris has cost him dearly," she said. "And you... you shall atone."

With a gesture, iron shackles formed around his wrists and ankles, binding him to the castle walls. "You are stripped of title and claim," she declared. "Henceforth, you are my servant. Mine a pure iron bar in this courtyard, day and night. A fruitless endeavor, for my glory alone. You shall never wander or explore; this castle is your world, this task your penance."

He struggled, but the iron held, his defiance crushed. The example was set: no lineage safe, opposition met with eternal servitude.

In the following days, the purge continued. Loyalists to Graf Albrecht—advisors, guards, even distant kin—were rounded up and brought to the courtyard. Valeska watched from a balcony, her face impassive as iron spikes extruded from the ground, impaling them slowly, their screams echoing as a warning. No maids were harmed; the servants who had toiled in silence were spared, their innocence acknowledged in Valeska's cold calculus. Previous prisoners, those languishing in the dungeons for petty theft or dissent, were released. Their chains melted like wax under her gaze, the iron reforming into harmless ornaments that they carried as tokens of freedom.

Valeska gathered the remaining court in the hall once more. "You have seen the cost of defiance," she said, her voice steady. "Now, choose. Stay, become strong under my protection, pledge your loyalty and allegiance to your queen. Or leave, and be anywhere else—but be warned: if you are not a citizen of Eisenburg, you are an enemy."

Many pledged, their voices trembling but sincere. Others left, vanishing into the night, their fates uncertain. Whispers of war spread, but Valeska's rule solidified.

Her first proclamation was sent out in all directions, carried by riders and ferromagni-enhanced messengers to landholders and kings alike: "I will rule all, and you will bow or I will take the very life you hold dear. Don't folly or believe this an idle threat. And to show you, in 5 nights I will commit conquest on Elgrabor, a city of slavers and depravity."

This was the spark of chapter 2, the beginning of her conquests.

As chapter 1 drew to a close, I, the observer, visited the courtyard one evening. The Barron's son, once robust and noble, was now a shadow—unshapely, just skin and bone, a ghost of his previous well-fed body. It saddened me, this proof that all can be broken, even the proudest bloodlines.
